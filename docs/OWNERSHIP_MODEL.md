# Ownership, operations and maintenance

SAANJH has four kinds of assets on a distribution transformer (DT):

1. smart meters with load limiting, already owned by the DISCOM under RDSS;
2. a gateway at the DT;
3. a community battery (second-life EV packs);
4. relay nodes on inverters, and actuators on appliances, at the few homes that offer
   flexibility.

Whoever owns them, the low-income households themselves pay nothing up front and nothing
monthly. The question is who pays for, runs and maintains the rest.

All figures below come from `simulation/economics_engine.py` (inputs and ranges in
`config/economics.yaml`) and are regenerated by `scripts/render_results.py`.

Reading the numbers:

- The **community battery** is most of the cost. The essential band and the inverter
  relays are cheap but, on a low-income DT, they cannot close deep shortfalls on their
  own (see the sensitivity table in the README).
- The DISCOM's **cash** benefits (energy it can still sell during shortfalls, flexibility
  credit toward its demand-flexibility obligation) do not repay the battery on their
  own. SAANJH is a reliability service with a cost. It should be judged by what it
  costs per household-hour of essential supply, against what families pay today to get
  the same hour from their own inverters, and against what a utility battery costs per
  kW.
- Lending a household's lead-acid inverter battery is expensive per kWh because of
  battery wear. Compensation is set above wear plus charging losses, so no lending
  household loses money. This is why SAANJH uses inverter relays only during
  shortfalls, never for routine peak shaving.

### A. DISCOM-owned

| | |
|---|---|
| Owns | Gateway, community battery, relay nodes and actuators (as distribution assets, alongside the smart meters it already owns) |
| Operates locally | A local operator (RWA volunteer, SHG member or electrician) on a monthly fee: enrolment, consent, first-line checks, household questions |
| Maintains | Battery: monthly visual and thermal check by the local operator, quarterly technician visit. Gateway and nodes: annual check, replacement on failure. Smart meters: existing DISCOM metering O&M |
| Cash flows | DISCOM pays capex, the operator fee, maintenance and household compensation; it keeps the energy-sales recovery and counts the flexibility toward its demand-flexibility obligation |
| Risks | Capex competes with other DISCOM priorities; a regulator must allow the battery into the asset base; DISCOM field staff are thin at DT level |

### B. Community-owned (RWA / cooperative / women's self-help group)

| | |
|---|---|
| Owns | The community battery and relay nodes, financed by a loan, and possibly a state or CSR grant |
| Operates locally | The community body itself |
| Maintains | Battery and nodes through a maintenance contract with the battery supplier; meters stay with the DISCOM |
| Cash flows | The DISCOM pays an availability fee per kW-month, set so the owner earns its required return, and passes household compensation through |
| Risks | Small owners carry battery and fire-safety risk; a higher cost of capital; needs a contract template the DISCOM's regulator accepts |

### C. Third-party aggregator

| | |
|---|---|
| Owns | Battery, gateway and nodes across many DTs |
| Operates locally | The aggregator's field staff, or subcontracted local operators |
| Maintains | The aggregator, under a service-level agreement on availability |
| Cash flows | The DISCOM pays an availability fee per kW-month; the aggregator registers under the state's demand-flexibility regulations (as Rajasthan's draft rules provide) |
| Risks | Highest cost of capital; the aggregator chases the most profitable DTs, which may not be the poorest |

## Recommendation

**DISCOM-owned assets, operated by a paid local operator from the community.**
Without an outside grant, the DISCOM-owned model gives the highest DISCOM NPV in both
scenarios, because the DISCOM's cost of capital is the lowest of the three. It also
keeps the flexibility credit with the party that carries the obligation. The community
still gets local jobs and a say in operations through the operator role.

A community-owned battery (B) only beats it when an outside capital grant covers part of
the cost. That is worth pursuing where a state or CSR scheme exists, but it moves cost
rather than removing it. `tests/test_economics.py` checks that this recommendation still
matches the computed numbers.
